No city in the county runs a system open to everyone, despite cities putting biking or bike share in their Climate Action Plans. A regional network turns those plans into bikes anyone can ride.

Docked vs. dockless

Docks rock

Docked stations are predictable, organized infrastructure that benefits everyone. They keep sidewalks clear and make sure bikes are maintained and available when you need one.

  • Predictable parking and organized public spaces
  • Reliable bike availability and consistent maintenance by experienced mechanics
  • A cohesive network that supports visitors, commuters, recreation and economic development
  • Clear operating standards across multiple jurisdictions

Why not dockless?

Dockless bikes and scooters end up wherever the last trip ended, often in the way of people who can’t move them. Virtual hubs can work in some places, but docked systems have a stronger track record of long-term success.

Bike share is simple and accessible

Walk up and unlock.

Find a station near the trolley, the coaster, your office or the beach and unlock an e-bike for your trip. No bike to buy, store, lock or fix.

Stations close the first and last mile, the stretch between a transit stop and where you’re really going, with connections to MTS and NCTD.

Let the motor take the hills.

The electric assist makes biking possible for more people. You go farther and handle San Diego’s hills with less effort, and you still get exercise.

New to riding in traffic? Our classes cover city commuting and safe e-bike riding. See classes

Dock it and walk away.

Return the bike to the same station or any other one in the network. Experienced mechanics keep the fleet maintained, so the next rider finds a bike that works.

One countywide system means your trip doesn’t stop at a city line.

Our gameplan

How we get there.

Five stops between today and a bike at your corner. Tap a stop.

Stop 1 · We are here

Build the coalition

Organizations sign the letter of support, neighbors sign the petition, and cities, agencies and campuses join the San Diego Regional Bike Share Alliance. This is the step that makes every other one possible.

Stop 2 · Next

A joint RFI

We are calling for a joint Request for Information to the bike share industry. We are asking governments, transit agencies, universities and employers to come together on it. It is a low-risk step that gets high-value answers before anyone signs a binding commitment, and it tells operators San Diego is serious and ready to collaborate. It would help partners evaluate:

  • Sponsorship and advertising potential
  • Operator financing capacity
  • Cost assumptions and service-level options
  • Long-term operating models that keep the system affordable
Stop 3 · Plan together

One MOU

Jurisdictions and partners work together through a Memorandum of Understanding: one coordinated planning process instead of a patchwork, which avoids duplication and gets the most from economies of scale.

Stop 4 · Buy together

One contract

One procurement pathway and one common contract structure that a wide range of agencies can execute. Less paperwork for every city, a better experience for riders, and a stronger hand with private partners.

Stop 5 · Estimated 2028

Wheels on the ground

One interoperable, countywide system with consistent standards for maintenance, customer service and performance reporting. Our petition estimates 2028.

Since you were probably wondering...

Who pays for it?

Bike share isn’t a money maker. It’s public transportation, and it needs a realistic funding plan. Ours rests on five pillars. Tap one.

Pillar 1

Corporate title sponsor

A multi-year sponsorship that anchors the program.

Pillar 2

Public-private partnership

Developer participation and public support, working together.

Pillar 3

Operator financing

The operator puts in capital to help launch and grow the system, not just run it.

Pillar 4

Employer and university subsidies

Employers and campuses support memberships for their people.

Pillar 5

Dedicated public funding

Targeted public investment fills the gaps and keeps access equitable, the way we already fund buses and trolleys.

Our position

Affordability isn’t optional.

Cities should subsidize memberships for residents, especially low-income riders, students and essential workers, so the system serves everyone and not just the people who could already afford a bike.

Climate action plans need bikes on the street.

Transportation is a major source of the region’s emissions. Jurisdictions, universities and agencies across the county have adopted Climate Action Plans that call for more biking, better transit access and fewer miles driven. Bike share delivers on them.

Who is in the San Diego Regional Bike Share Alliance?

A growing group of regional leaders, including the City of San Diego’s Climate Advisory and Mobility Boards, who believe in bike share and want to see it become real in San Diego. Organizations join by signing our letter of support.

What is the difference between docked and dockless?

Dockless devices don’t lock into a dock at a station. Virtual hubs can work in some places, but docked systems have a stronger track record of long-term success and keep sidewalks clear for people who can’t move dockless devices out of the way.

Why one regional system instead of city-by-city programs?

A coordinated regional system costs less to run, works better for riders who cross city lines, and makes San Diego a more attractive partner for operators and sponsors. One system means more bikes, more stations and more value for everyone.

How will low-income residents be able to use it?

Affordability is central. The funding model includes dedicated public funding and employer or university subsidies for reduced-cost memberships for low-income riders, students and essential workers.

When will bike share launch?

We’re in the coalition-building and RFI phase now. Signing the petition or letter of support and joining the Alliance are the most important steps today. Our newsletter will share timeline updates as the program moves forward.
